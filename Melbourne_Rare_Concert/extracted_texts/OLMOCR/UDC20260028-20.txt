ALTO SOLO—MISS ADELAIDE BRUCE.

Life presented itself to the Greek mind as a series of manifestations of Power. Omnipotent, irresistible, irrevocable, first in the order of forces, loomed Fate, then the gods, the ancestral heroes, and the pre-eminent men of the times. Each State, surrounded by enemies, depended for its safety on the courage and power of its citizens, whom a reverse of war would convert into slaves with their wives and children. The actual experience of the Power and Tragedy of human existence, which to our modern comfortable plebian semi-vegetable life are almost unintelligible, lent an intensity to the moment which we can but faintly imagine. Power and its limitation were equally displayed in the sudden plunge from the height of prosperity to the depth of woe, and found expression in the never equalled strength and restraint of Greek art, imparting a reverential solemnity even to its most joyous utterances. This is the key note of their art, energetic power, and fate:

Fate, whose horizons, narrowing from afar
Within their blind abysmal night immerse
Both gods and men, and all things else that be
In irremediable catastrophe.

In the following ode from Goethe's masterpiece, these qualities are passionately felt by one who was himself a Greek at heart though born in a barbarian age. The music is an endeavour to complete and enforce the poet's intention. The serenity, not untinged with a certain noble sadness, of the first stanza, is contrasted with those following, in which the power and majesty and terrible presence of the gods is imaged, which succeeds the picture of limited fettered humanity.

SONG—Die Lorelei F. Liszt.
Orchestrated by Professor Marshall-Hall.

MISS MARGUERITE HENDERSON.

I KNOW not what it betokens
That I am so sad at heart,
A legend from bygone ages
Will not from my mind depart.
The air is cool and it darkens,
And calmly flows the Rhine,
The mountain's far summit sparkles
In gentle evening shine.
A lovely lady sitteth
Thereby, entrancing fair,
Her golden raiment glistens,
She combs her golden hair.
She combs it with comb all golden,
And sings a song the while,
That has a soul-betwitching
Resistless melody.
The boatman, as onward he fareth,
It seizeth with anguish dread,
He sees not the rocks fast shoaling,
He looks ever up overhead.
Meseems the wavelets swallow
At last both boatman and boat,
And this with her fatal singing
The Loreley has wrought.

His horsemen hard behind us ride;
    Should they our steps discover,
Then who will cheer my bonny bride,
    When they have slain her lover?
Out spake the hardy highland wight,
    "I'll go, my chief, I'm ready!
It is not for your silver bright,
    But for your winsome lady.
And by my word, the bonny bird
    In danger shall not tarry,
So though the waves are raging white,
    I'll row you o'er the ferry."
By this the storm grew loud apace,
    The water wraith was shrieking,
And in the scowl of heaven each face
    Grew dark as they were speaking.
But still as wilder blew the wind,
    And as the night grew drearer,
Adown the glen rode armed men,
    Their tramping sounded nearer.
"Oh haste thee, haste!" the lady cries,
    "Though storms around us gather,
I'll meet the raging of the skies,
    But not an angry father."
The boat has left the stormy land,
    A stormy sea before her,
When, oh, too strong for human hand,
    The tempest gather'd o'er her.
And still they rowed amidst the roar
    Of waters fast prevailing:
Lord Ullin reached the fatal shore,
    His wrath was changed to wailing.
For sore dismayed, thro' storm and shade,
    He did his child discover:
One lovely arm she stretched for aid,
    And one was round her lover.
"Come back, come back!" he cried in grief
    Across the stormy water,
And I'll forgive your Highland chief,
    My daughter! oh, my daughter!"
