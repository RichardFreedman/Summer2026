"Now haud your tongue o' my love and vows,
For they can breed but strife;
Now haud your tongue o' my former vows,

"O fause are the vows o' womankind,
But fair is their fause bodie;
I wad never hae trodden on Irish ground,
Were it no for the love o' thee."

"Ye may leave your husband to himself,
And your little son also,
And sail wi' me across the sea,
Sae fair the wind doth blow."

"See ye not seven pretty ships,
The eighth brought me to land,
With merchandise and mariners,
And music on every hand?"

"There's mantles warm to wrap my love,
O' the silk and soft velvet,
And rich attires to deck her head,
And costly shoon for her feet."

"O how do you like the ship?" he said,
"Or how do you like the sea?
And how do you like the bold mariners
That wait upon thee and me?"

"O weel I like the ship," she said,
"And weel I like the sea,
But where are a' your mariners?
I see nane but thee and me."

They hadna sailed a league, a league,
A league, but barely three,
When eerie grew the lift above,
And gurly grew the sea.

She hadna sailed a league, a league,
A league, but barely three,
When she espied the cloven hoof,
And wept right bitterlie.

"O what are you, yon pleasant hills,
That the sun shines sweetly on?"
"O you are the hills of heaven," he said
"Where you will never win."

=== Page 9 ===
"O whatna mountain is yon," she said,
"Sae dreary wi' frost and snow?"
"O yon is the mountain o' hell," he cried,
"Where you and I maun go."

The clouds grew black, and the wind grew loud,
And the levin filled her e'e;
And wesome wailed the snow-white sprites,
Out o'er the roaring sea.

He struck the mainmast wi' his hand,
The foremast wi' his knee;
He split the gallant ship in twain,
And sank her in the sea.

ALTO SOLO—MISS SPENCE.

Life presented itself to the Greek mind as a series of manifestations of Power Omnipotent, irresistible, irrevocable, first in the order of forces, loomed Fate, then the gods, the ancestral heroes, and the pre-eminent men of the times. Each State, surrounded by enemies, depended for its safety on the courage and power of its citizens, whom a reverse of war would convert into slaves with their wives and children. The actual experience of the Power and Tragedy of human existence, which to our modern comfortable plebian semi-vegetable life are almost unintelligible, lent an intensity to the moment which we can but faintly imagine. Power and its limitation were equally displayed in the sudden plunge from the height of prosperity to the depth of woe, and to find expression in the never equalled strength and restraint of Greek art, imparting a reverential solemnity even to its most joyous utterances. This is the key note of their art, energetic power, and fate:

Fate, whose horizons, narrowing from afar
Within their blind abysmal night immerse
Both gods and men, and all things else that be
In irremediable catastrophe.

In the following ode from Goethe's masterpiece, these qualities are passionately felt by one who was himself a Greek at heart though born in a barbarian age. The music is an endeavour to complete and enforce the poet's intention. The serenity, not untinged with a certain noble sadness, of the first stanza, is contrasted with those following, in which the power and majesty and terrible presence of the gods is imaged, to which succeeds the picture of limited fettered humanity.

ORGAN SOLO—
Passacaglia
J. S. Bach.
MR. AUGUST SIEDE.

In the Passacaglia we see, from a single idea by a development gradual, but resistless as fate, the growth of a huge superstructure almost overpowering in its nobility and grandeur, suggesting the unfolding of some mighty work of nature herself.
When the watery ear-tickling organ music of the present age will have vanished into oblivion, such works as this will stand as the eternal monument to the imperishable glory of Bach.

Hungarian Dances
J. Brahms.
No. 1—Allegro molto.
No. 2—Allegretto.
ORCHESTRA.

In the wonderfully expressive and characteristic music of the Hungarians we find preserved the nobler and more significant phases of the Dance, before it had been corrupted by civilised larrkinism, and whilst it still remained among the people at large the one means through which their natural feelings and instincts found expression.

=== Page 15 ===
Not only at one time was the Dance a religious rite, but also it fulfilled the present office of the drama, and became an artistic representation of human passion and action. In the course of the various figures through which the dancers went, the joy and woe, love and hate, hope and despair, of mankind were represented. Youths would woo and maidens would play the coquette.

In Brahms' music we find not only those poetical moods which it once was the object of the dance to portray, but a delightful suggestion of the picturesque character of the people, and the free, wild naive aspect of Nature, which nowhere assumes a more beautiful or striking aspect than in the country of the Maygars.

MISS ADELAIDE DONOVAN.

If a youth should meet a maiden,
    Need she run away with fright;
If his looks should win her favour,
    Sure the girl may use her sight.
Tho' she must, in maiden manner,
    Seem her glances to conceal;
Where's the harm, if she, unnoticed,
    Strive a sidelong look to steal.
Should perchance their eyes encounter,
    I no guilt therein can find;
Tho' her cheeks may blush a little,
    Yet it will not strike her blind.
Looking hither, looking thither,
    Looks are followed soon by sighs;
He sighs fairest, she smiles dearest,
    Soon a marriage we will see.
Come around me, dear companions,
    Would you like to see me bride?
Am I not a pretty maiden,
    And he is a smart young boy.

BALLAD—

Lord Ullin's Daughter
Hamish MacCunn.

THE LIEDERTAFEL, LADIES CHOIR
AND ORCHESTRA.

A CHIEFTAIN to the highlands bound
    Cries "Boatman, do not tarry!
And I'll give thee a silver pound
    To row us o'er the ferry."

    "Now who be ye would cross Lochgyle
    This dark and stormy water?"
    "Oh, I'm the chief of Ulvas' Isle,
    And this, Lord Ullin's daughter."

And fast before her father's men,
    Three days we've fled together,
And should he find us in the glen,
    My blood would stain the heather.

His horsemen hard behind us ride;
    Should they our steps discover,
Then who will cheer my bonny bride,
    When they have slain her lover?

Out spake the hardy highland wight,
    "I'll go, my chief, I'm ready!
It is not for your silver bright,
    But for your winsome lady.

And by my word, the bonny bird
    In danger shall not tarry,
So though the waves are raging white,
    I'll row you o'er the ferry."

By this the storm grew loud apace,
    The water wraith was shrieking,
And in the scowl of heaven each face
    Grew dark as they were speaking.

But still as wilder blew the wind,
    And as the night grew drearer,
Adown the glen rode armed men,
    Their tramping sounded nearer.

=== Page 17 ===
"Oh haste thee, haste!" the lady cries,
    "Though storms around us gather,
I'll meet the raging of the skies,
    But not an angry father."

The boat has left the stormy land,
    A stormy sea before her,
When, oh, too strong for human hand,
    The tempest gather'd o'er her.

And still they rowed amidst the roar
    Of waters fast prevailing:
Lord Ullin reached the fatal shore,
    His wrath was changed to wailing.

For sore dismayed, thro' storm and shade,
    He did his child discover:
One lovely arm she stretched for aid,
    And one was round her lover.

"Come back, come back!" he cried in grief
    Across the stormy water,
And I'll forgive your Highland chief,
    My daughter! oh, my daughter!"

'Twas vain: the loud waves lashed the shore,
    Return or aid preventing,
The waters wild went o'er his child,
    And he was left lamenting.
