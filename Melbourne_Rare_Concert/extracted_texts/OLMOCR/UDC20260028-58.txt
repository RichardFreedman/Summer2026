The following notes are principally taken from Masters of German Music, by Mr. J. A. Fuller Maitland.

The Requiem, which was finished in 1868, and first produced, in a complete form, at Bremen, on Good Friday in that year, was the first work which established Brahms' fame, and has been described as "the greatest achievement of modern sacred music in Germany." It is not a "Requiem" in the ordinary acceptation of the term. It is in reality a Sacred Cantata, composed to words selected from the Holy Scripture, in illustration of the joys of the blessed and the glories of the life to come. It exhibits throughout a striking originality. In his desire to shadow forth the glories of a higher state of existence, the composer has availed himself of the latest resources of modern music, including the most complicated orchestral effects, and choral passages of almost unconquerable difficulty.

The next number (II.) opens with a Funeral March. The idea is that of the whole human race marching in solemn procession to the grave, and this idea has inspired Brahms to the composition of a march unlike all marches that were ever written. It is in triple-time—the measured tramp of no ordinary host. The effect is heightened by the employment of unison in the vocal parts at the words "Behold all flesh is as the grass." The imagery of these words, perhaps connected with the "sowing in tears" of the preceding chorus, suggests the passage from the Epistle of St. James, "The husbandman waiteth for the precious fruit of the earth"; and this leads to the beautiful fugal setting of the words "The redeemed of the Lord shall return." The repetition of the words "joy everlasting," with which the chorus closes, is a most striking contrast to the gloom of the beginning.

The third number (III.) starts with a Baritone Solo, "Lord make me to know," melodious, expressive, and pre-eminently vocal. The Chorus then repeat the same words, as also in the next section, "Verily mankind walketh in a vain show." At the words "Now, Lord, O what do I wait for," the voice parts become more involved and the music more agitated, leading up to a very fine climax at the words "My hope is in Thee." Here the four sections of the choir enter successively from the lowest register of the bass to the high soprano, suggesting the idea of a hope built upon a sure foundation, and leading into the splendid fugue "But the righteous souls are in the hand of God," in which the same impression of stability and permanence is conveyed, and is indeed accentuated by its being constructed throughout one continuing Pedal note.

After the assurance as to the destiny of the "righteous souls," it is natural to turn to the joys of their abode, and in the next number (IV.) "How lovely is thy dwelling place!" the perfect peace of heaven is reflected. Attention may be drawn to the fine fugal treatment of the words "They praise Thy Name evermore," towards the end of this movement.

The subject of the next number (V.) is that of comfort to mourners, based on the happy state of the holy dead. The loss of the composer's mother is said to have determined its form, which is that of a soprano solo, accompanied by a quiet chorus, which repeats the words "As one whom his own mother comforteth, so will I comfort you."

The sixth chorus (VI.) begins with a passage of simple four-part harmony, "Here we have no continuing place," and the baritone solo entering at the words "Lo! I unfold unto you a mystery," the thoughts are led on to the change from mortality to immortality, on which the burial service lays stress. To English ears the re-setting of "For the trumpet shall sound," &c., may seem almost sacrilegious; but it must be remembered that the Messiah is far less familiar in Germany than here. The dramatic passages descriptive of the "mystery" serve as a preparation for the calm dignity and devotional grandeur of the double fugue, "Lord, Thou art worthy."

RHAPSODIE.
(FRAGMENT AUS GETHE'S "HARZREISE IM WINTER.")
FOR
CONTRALTO SOLO, CHORUS OF MEN'S VOICES, AND ORCHESTRA.

BY JOHANNES BRAHMS (Op. 53)

Royal Victorian Liedertafel and Orchestra
SOLOIST—MADAME AGNES JANSON.

The deep majestic expression which the greatest musical classic of our time has given to the words of Goethe, establishes the work under notice as one of the highest manifestations of lofty subjectivity and profound contemplation that we worship in art. The Solo voice is responsible for the text throughout. The entry of Tenors and Basses—each in two parts—is made at the words, "But if from thy Psalter."

JOHANNES BRAHMS, born 1833 at Hamburg; as a boy excited great attention both by compositions and pianoforte playing. In 1874, he was made a member of the Academy of Arts at Berlin. Excepting opera, Brahms cultivated nearly every branch of musical composition. Powerful, original and versatile, Brahms, more than any other, sums up in his strongly-marked individuality the varying elements: classical as well as romantic, of contemporaneous culture. His imagination, to which he at first allowed too free a scope (about to Op. 10), he bridled by severe study and rigid self-criticism; and although reflection plays a large part in his compositions, he produced abundance of music of a thoroughly spontaneous and inspired character. His melodies possess inexhaustible variety, ranging from naive simplicity and a dreamy poetical tenderness peculiar to himself, to stirring pathos, striking force, and glowing passion; always remaining pregnant and concise. His harmony, often surprisingly bold and original, is subordinated to the dictates of a high artistic idea, which permeates and controls the formal construction of his works, in which an admirable mastery of contrapuntal art is often exhibited. His songs extend from the simplest specimens of popular melody to the richest and most complicated forms of art.
