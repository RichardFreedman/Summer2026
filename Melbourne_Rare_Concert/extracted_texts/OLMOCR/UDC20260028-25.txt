Cantata—"The Maid of Orleans"
Heinrich Hofmann

English Version by Mrs. John P. Morgan

Heinrich Josef Franz Hofmann, who was born at Berlin in 1842, first came into notice as a rising musician by his "Hungarian Suite" for orchestra, which was produced in 1873. This met with an enthusiastic reception throughout Germany; and the composer followed up his initial success in the following year with a symphony to which he gave the name "Frithjof," while in 1875 he brought out a cantata on the subject of "Die schöne Melusine," both of these works displaying his powers in a highly favourable light. The work performed this evening is an admirable example of his style, which though it still bears traces of the influence of other composers, notably Wagner, and occasionally Bizet, will be generally admitted to be characterized by melodic gifts of a high order, coupled with mastery over the resources of orchestral colour, and imbued throughout with true dramatic feeling.

Part I.—A brief orchestral prelude—sostenuto—leads to a spirited chorus wherein the pleasures of the vintage and the virtues of wine are duly set forth. This joyous strain is interrupted by the arrival of a band of peasants with tidings of the ravages caused by the conquering English troops, and all

=== Page 10 ===
bewail their own weakness and the restless might of the foe. Joan appears, and after declaring her supernatural mission, breaks into a triumphant song in which the chorus, infected by her dauntless spirit, subsequently join. The stirring melody of this number, with its fine instrumentation and the effective use made of the harp, cannot fail to appeal to all who hear it.

PART II.—After a few characteristic bars of introduction, the King welcomes his soldiers, who have brought Joan to him, clad in full armour. In answer to his inquiry, the King is told that when the troops were on the point of yielding, this young maid had come to their assistance, and that by her aid they had routed the enemy. Joan's solo, telling of the mysterious command she had received whilst praying before a picture of the Madonna in her native village, is preceded by a striking passage from the clarionet, and the opening subject in A minor is accompanied alternatively with the woodwind and the muted strings; the same motif is then given in the major, an almost martial character being here imparted to it by the use of trumpets. In answer to further questions from the King, Joan foretells that she will bring him victorious to Rheims, and the army with a shout demands that she shall at once lead them against the foe. This concluding number of Part II. is a finely-written double chorus, the two divisions, after a brief duet for Joan and the King, uniting in an ensemble which, from a musical standpoint, is undoubtedly the climax of the whole work.

PART III.—Joan of Arc, betrayed into the hands of the English, has miraculously escaped, and once more led her countrymen to victory, but at the cost of a fatal wound. The French soldiers, believing her to be dead, sing a solemn funeral march; but reviving a little, she asks where she is, and is surprised to find herself with her friends. The cantata concludes with the death of Joan, who gives utterance to her belief in the everlasting joys in store for her, while the chorus indulge in lamentations at the untimely death that thus overtakes the saviour of her country.

SECOND PART.

King.—My welcome to ye, noble warriors!
    What bring ye me? have tidings had?
    And who is yonder lovely maid,
    In glittering armour clad?

Warriors' Chorus.—
    We would, o'ercome in bloody battle,
        Our host surrender to its foes,
    When lo, before us, our eyes beheld it,
        A sudden, mighty wonder rose.
    For from the wood toward us gliding,
        Darted a virgin glorious fair,
    Adorned with armour of a goddess,
        The battle watching there.
    A holy light from heavenly height
        O'ershone her armour, splendour rife,
    And with strong hand she caught high our banner,
        And led us on to war and strife.
    Then fled our foemen in wildest tumult,
        No leader's voice they hear or know;
    From terror dazed, plunge man and horse,
        O'er rocks into the stream below.
    And so we won the awful fight,
        By this fair virgin's wondrous might.

King.—How marvellous!
    Who art thou, mighty presence?—speak!

Joan.—Only a shepherd's child am I.

King.—And how hath God thee victor crowned?

Chorus.—Speak! How hath God us victors crowned?

Joan.—Near my home, my native village,
    Stands an old Madonna shrine;
    And there, on my knees, I oft have knelt,
    In fervent, ardent prayer.
    And I prayed to God's own mother:
    O, avert the tide of war,
    And in mercy guard from danger
    Our King for evermore.

Warriors' Chorus.—

    Comrades, mourn for the glorious maiden;
    She that us from our foes hath saved,

    With thine own folk, with thine own people.

Joan.—No, I am no enchantress, believe me I am not!

    And our dull eyes were but with night bedimmed.

Part Song—"The Two Roses" Werner

Liedertafel

On a bank two roses fair,
    Wet with morning showers,
        Filled with dew in fragrance grew,
As I, pensive, full of care,
    Gathered two sweet flowers.
        Tell me roses, truly tell
        If my fair one loves me well.

This in leaves of white array'd,
    Not a speck to dim them,
        So I find the spotless mind
Which adorns my lovely maid—
    Innocence's emblem.
        Tell me then, and truly tell
        If my fair one loves me well.

Like her cheeks, the blushing ray
    Which thy bud encloses,
        Brighter far than you they are;
But her charms if I should say,
    Ye'll be jealous, roses.
        Tell me then, and truly tell
        If my fair one loves me well.

Vocal March—"The Tyrolese Hunters"
Roschat
Liedertafel and Orchestra

HALLO! Hallo!
Now hunters come away, for early breaks the day;
With rifles prov'd and tried we range the mountain side,
Keep distance every man, each nook and covert scan:
From by the forest end to where the two ways blend.

Yoho, yoho, yoho! There something crouches low;
What eyes can shine like that? It is a mountain cat.
'Tis only so I fear: It is a crouching deer;
Surround it every way, before it steals away.

See now he springs and flies, and for the forest tries,
But there the hunters stand, with steady eye and hand.
And crack it goes, yoho! the ball has laid him low;
A noble beast is he, and killed most manfully.

Here from the waterfall we send a hunter's call,
To glad the list'ning ear of wives and sweethearts dear.
So gently timid they, that if we shoot they say—
"Ah me! there's someone hurt to-day."

O sweethearts in the vale, you from afar we hail,
With joyful notes that tell that all with us is well,
Yet here awhile we roam: and with the evening come
To seek our dear and quiet home.

Tra la, la la, la la! then there's hurrah, hurrah!
For such a bonny land, for vales and mountains grand.
And here's hurrah, hurrah! for wives and maidens true.
Sweethearts, we shout for you, hurrah!

An eye that never fails, a heart that never quails,
A cheek aglow with health, is more than idle wealth.
Hallo! hallo, hallo! a-hunting we will go;
By topmost crag and peak our timid prey we seek,
Hallo! hallo, hallo! a-hunting we will go:
Full quickly flies the day: Hallo, hallo, away!

Song—"When Sorrow Sleepeth"
Land-Elsässer
(WITH HUMMING ACCOMPANIMENT)
Signor Buzzi and Liedertafel

When sorrow sleepeth, wake it not,
    But let it slumber on;
If grief is for a while forgot,
    Its power that while is gone.
The mind may from the pause gain strength
    To grapple with its foe,
And thence may rise to prove, at length,
    Triumphant over woe.
When sorrow sleepeth, wake it not,
    O wake it not.

To mourn the lost, we sigh for care,
    We grieve, by sin opprest;
And all a secret burden bear,
    For earth is not our rest.
Then watch thy thoughts, thy words restrain,
    Each heart its burden knows;
One little word, all light and vain,
    May break that heart's repose.
When sorrow sleepeth, wake it not,
    O wake it not.
