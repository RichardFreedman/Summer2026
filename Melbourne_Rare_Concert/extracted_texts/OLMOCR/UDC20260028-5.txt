During the winter of 1968-69, CROSS TALK will sponsor the first festival of live electronic music in Japan. Several guest composer/engineers from the United States are expected. In addition, general demonstrations for the public; special work sessions on basic technical resources (e.g., ring modulators, photo cells, computer applications, etc.); open rehearsals; experimental presentations; and public events are planned.

Later in the spring of 1969, several days of intermedia events will be held, including performances of IANNIS XENAKIS' Terretektorn for 88 players seated among a circularly arranged audience; JOHN CAGE's as yet incomplete HPSCHD for fifty computer-derived tapes and harpsichord soloist; and the premiere of Flight-Operatic by TORU TAKEMITSU, for orchestra, mime, dancers, film, tape, and so on.

Various other events on a smaller scale will be presented from time to time, as announced.

Distributions（分布）へのノート

1966～67年にかけて作曲。前方から弦楽四重奏が、そして後方からは四重奏中の2パートをリング変調した音響がきこえてくる。
次の諸要素から成る。
O：弦楽器の調弦を模した短かい部分でAとCを内包している。
A：やや長い持続のクラスター。徐々にDに近づく。
B：アルペジオ音型。複雑化されたCとDに似た性格をおびる。
C：グリッサンド。Dに接近する。
D：騒音、極端な状態では事実上Eに等しい。聴覚の分解能力を考えるならば、極度に複雑なものは、かえって単純なものとし
て理解されるからである。
E：間（ま）
各要素は、その持続や出現順序に関してボアソン分布や正規分布などのような分布に従っている。全体的には単純→複雑という傾向に配列されているが、この複雑化の過程で各要素はまったく逆の性質をもったものに変質してしまう（例：A→D、B→C & D、C→DそしてD→E）。最後は最も複雑な要素がおかれているので、聴覚的には単純→複雑→単純という形式をもつようにきこえる。
（松平頼曉）

飯村隆彦

1937年生れの実験映画作家。1960年に慶応大学を卒業後、映画の制作に専心。1964年のブリュッセル実験映画祭では、日本の若い一群の作家たちの作品がそろって受賞したが、そのなかには、かれの〈オナン〉も含まれていた。同年、フィルム・アンデパンダンを組織し、日本のアンダー・グラウンド・フィルムの活動をつづけた。1966年、ハーバード大学の国際セミナーに招かれて渡米。

代表作としては、〈オナン〉〈ラヴ〉〈デリバット王国の舞踏会〉などがある。

〈シェルター9999〉について

この作品はアルヴィン・ルシエと渡米中の飯村隆彦の近作の共同作品。4つの要素が同時的にパフォーマンスされていく。その4要素とはまず35分の黒白フィルム。それにループになった4本のエンドレスの黒白フィルム。これには戦術的な意味でパンチがいれられて穴があけられている。さらにこの他にアクチュアルな問題をとらえて撮影した12枚のカラー・スライドが併映される。そしてこれらの映像にたいして、4チャンネルの音響がかみあわされていくが、これはかならずしも映像とシンクロナイズする意図があたえられてはいない。

これらの映像は指定にしたがって上映されていくが、それぞれがほとんど同時にダブって映写されていく。そしてその場合、色彩フィルターとレンズによって、これらの映像が変形されつづける。

テープの音は、3次元の方向性をもつ物体の振動のうちで、とくに一方向の振動についてのみ敏感にキャッチし、とりだした音によっている。こうしてさまざまな視覚的、聴覚的な繰り返しがおこなわれていくのだが、その組み合わせがいつも変化していくことによって、強烈な印象とサイケデリックな効果がうみだされていくのである。

(AUSTIN, BABBITT)

LARRY AUSTIN was born in Oklahoma in 1930. He studied composition with Darius Milhaud, Andrew Imbrie, and Seymour Shifrin while at North Texas State University and the University of California, at Berkeley. Since 1958, he has taught at the University of California, Davis, and formed the New Music Ensemble (NME) with his colleagues there. This group of composer/performers is particularly devoted to evolving new concepts of group improvisation. Mr. Austin has also been instrumental in organizing a music publishing service "Composer/Performer Edition" (CPE) and edits the unique avant garde music magazine, Source. His works include: Improvisations for Orchestra and Jazz Soloists (1963); The Maze for three percussionists, dancer, tape, machines, and projections (1966); Changes for trombone and tape (1966); Accidents for electronically prepared piano, tape, mirrors, actions, and projections (1967); and Bass, a theater piece in open style for contrabass, tape, and film (1967). Mr. Austin's compositions are published by MJQ Music, Inc. and by CPE, and are recorded on Columbia and NME Records.

BRASS was commissioned by the American Brass Quintet with the aid of a research grant from the University of California, Davis. It was filmed in New York City during September of 1967 at the Merce Cunningham Foundation dance studio. Brass is dedicated to my instrument, the trumpet. It was conceived as a silent movie with live electronic sound. Rather than hearing a prerecorded sound track, the movie-goer hears, in actual performance, live electronic music created by the cast (the American Brass Quintet) at the same time the movie is being shown. The sounds are produced by means of special electronically amplified mutes, which make it possible for the audience to hear instrumental sounds that would normally be inaudible beyond a few feet. These "micro-sounds" are electronically modified and finally amplified through speakers in the hall. The players "play" the movie and the slides. They receive some information about what to play from their own images on the screen (density, articulation, texture, timing, instrumentation, timbre, etc.) and the remainder (frequency, intensity, envelope, rhythm, event sequence, etc.) from the color slides of brass tablatures superimposed over the film. Except for the first and last, the slides are faded in and out at random intervals in any order.

The stereophonic tape used at this concert was taken from the November 6, 1967 performance by the American Brass Quintet, and sound modification was achieved by means of Buchla Associates Electronic Modular System equipment. There should be no attempt to synchronize the tape and film.

-Larry Austin

MILTON BABBITT was born in Philadelphia in 1916, and entered New York University in 1931 as a student of mathematics and logic. The impact of Schoenberg and Webern redirected his interest towards music, however, and he began study in composition with Marion Bauer and Philip James in New York. Later he worked with Roger Sessions at Princeton, obtaining his MA in 1942. Mr. Babbitt has taught both mathematics and music at Princeton, where he has been on the faculty since 1938. He is co-director of the Columbia-Princeton Center for Electronic Music. As the leading American theoretician for the post-Webern school, he has written numerous technical articles on serial procedures. His compositions include: Composition for Twelve Instruments (1948); two string quartets (1948 and 1954); Composition for Tenor and Six Instruments (1958-60); Composition for Synthesizer, generated on the RCA Electronic Sound Synthesizer (1961); Ensembles for Synthesizer (1961-63); and Philomel for soprano, recorded soprano, and synthesized sound (1963). A number of Mr. Babbitt's works are available from Associated Music Publishers, and his music is recorded on Columbia and Composers Recordings Incorporated.

COMPOSITION FOR FOUR INSTRUMENTS was composed in 1947-48 and is the second of six works entitled "compositions" extending over a fifteen-year period (the first was Three Compositions for Piano, 1946-47). I chose this term not only to avoid titles with historical or formal connotations, and to suggest (in order to prepare the listener for) a certain degree of abstractness and formalism, but primarily to provide information about the musical "language" employed and specific characteristics of the individual work. In the former case, the term "composition" is associated with the operations on permutations corresponding to a familiar representation of the customary transformations of the twelve-tone system; while in the latter case the term is applied to partitions in which the order of parts is significant, corresponding musically to the registral and timbral partitioning of the twelve-tone aggregate: the unit of ensemble progression, whose "parts" are always segments of a twelve-tone set, and, therefore an ordered pitch collection.

Composition for Four Instruments is the second work I completed after a war-enforced, six-year respite from composition. Before and during this period I was engrossed in the compositional extension and generalization of the Schoenbergian principle of "combinationality," and the Webernian technique of set construction by derivation. In this piece, the twelve-tone set is never presented in an explicit manner. The sets that constitute the pitch material of the individual instruments are all "derived" (by the three operations of the twelve-tone system) from the four discrete hexachords of the work's set, and share a common hexachordal combinationality with each other and with the set of the work. The opening clarinet solo begins by exposing an all-interval set, which functions thereafter as a foreground thematic set. It is produced by a registral delineation of the compositional set's opening trichord and three transformations that form, together, the first of the eight aggregates making up this section. From an awareness of this aspect of the work's structure, its most immediate design characteristic - the fifteen instrumentally defined sections - is observed to function as a large-scale analogue to that disposition of trichords.

Composition for Four Instruments is also the second of my completed works in which the temporal dimension is structured in terms of the formation and transformational principles of the twelve-tone pitch-class system. Thus, it is not just serialized but twelve-tone serialized. It embodies not just a contextual analogy, but a compositional definition of the temporal elements of attack point and metrical organization. Time perception is scaled "intervallically" (with duration functioning as temporal interval) and, thereby, invariants under the operations of the system assume an empirical status in the time domain, one that is closely comparable to their position in the pitch domain.

- Milton Babbitt

シェーンベルクの12音技法から、ミュージック・セリエルや電子音楽を発展させた、ヨーロッパ近代文化を「正統」とする立場から、今日いわば、正統的前衛主義者と考えられている作家達の「思考」、すなわち「作曲技法」においては、一般的傾向として、「音」は実在的な「音」である前に、抽象的な「音高」として、「音価」として、「強弱」として、あるいは「音色」として在らねばならなかった。そして更に、このような「パラメーター」の総体として観念的に規定された「音」は、それ自体、自立的に存在し得るのではなく、より上位の構造を構成するための「素材」としてしか在ることができなかった。勿論、「技法」が一種の「便法」である以上、このことのみを取り上げてこれらの作家達の行き方を全面的に否定するつもりは全くない。しかし、私自身が求める「音」は、主として数理的な思考と操作によってその存在の場を保証されて在るような「音」ではない。あたかも自ら息づきつつその実在の瞬間を、深く埋めて在るような「音」なのだ「2人の奏者のための〈場〉」で、私は「音」がそのような「在り方」をする〈場〉を構成しようと意図した。

ROBERT MORRIS was born in Cheltenham, England in 1943. He received his BM from the Eastman School of Music and an MM from the University of Michigan. Presently completing his Doctorate in Composition and Ethnomusicology at the University of Michigan where he is a teaching fellow, he has studied with Ross Lee Finney, Leslie Bassett, Niccolo Castiglioni, and Eugene Kurtz. At the Berkshire Music Center (Tanglewood) as a Margaret Lee Crofts Fellow, he worked with Gunther Schuller, and in 1965, the University of Syracuse commissioned him to write music for Max Frisch's Biederman and the Firebugs. He also participated in the Rockefeller Foundation's program for orchestral readings by the Baltimore Symphony Orchestra. In conjunction with Russell Peck, William Albright, and David Andrew, Mr. Morris co-founded The Great Society, a theater group in Ann Arbor. His works include: Frondescence 65 for flute and piano; Sangita 67 for any number of flutes, strings, and percussion; Dark Halo for Orchestra; Night Crawlers for tape and dancers; Denkmaeler, theater event; Forgotten Vibrations for ten instruments and soprano; and Capricorn 68 for nine to infinity players, with at least three conductors.

In the history of art music, the listener's interest within some understood set of rules, traditions, proprieties, forms, etc., is what will happen, that is, the relationship of predictable to unpredictable elements in a piece of music. If the music is improvised (instead of notated descriptively or in any way), the performer also becomes involved as a listener. His perception of what he is playing acts as a feedback governing how or what he will play next.

In Western Music from 1600-1950 there has been the figure of the composer as an autonomous personality who structures the whole musical situation so that the players become ultimately mere machines translating a notational system into an aural one. In such a position the composer can never become a listener except when he is actually composing, since once the piece is completed, he "knows" it (in the sense that he has totally determined it).

The nearer we get to the present the more crucial the problem becomes, and it seems to me that the composer's problem in the last ten years has been how to become a listener again without giving up composition.

NOTES FROM THE UNDERGROUND (1966) is one of a series of pieces that employs indeterminacy as a structural device. The sounds used are arranged in a continuum from clearly recognizable pitches to ambiant noises. The vocal elements are likewise disposed from purely phonetic sounds to an almost phonemic section based on the permutation of the letters of a five-letter word. A semi-spacial notation is used, placing events within sections of various lengths. The result will be progressively more unpredictable the longer the section.

I continued this train of thought in subsequent works (Sangita 67, Next to Last Rites, and Capricorn 68) where notation or a prerecorded tape serves as a static, determinant element from which the performers can interpret/improvise. I hoped to bring to these pieces that special quality of the unforeseen, without trespassing the boundaries I had originally conceived.

- Robert Morris

AKIMICHI TAKEDA was born in 1937, and graduated in musicology from Tokyo University of the Arts in 1962. Beginning at this time, he participated with Takehisa Kosugi, Shuko Mizuno, Yasunao Tone, and Chieko Shiomi in the "Group Ongaku" (Music Group). He has been teaching music history at the Musashino Music Academy since 1965, and is presently working on a collective article "Sound and Thought" carried in installments by the Japanese magazine Ongaku Geijutsu. His compositions include: Changes for string instruments (1963); Passages for unspecified performer(s) (1964); and Open Sounds for tape (1965).

If one views the development of modern European music from Schoenberg through serialism and electronic music as legitimate, one can say that, for Europeans, tone is above all an abstract entity characterized by pitch, duration, intensity, and tone color. There is another European supposition: that the overall tonal form is a result of relationships between these parameters, and that sound itself can only be thought of as raw material by means of which a structure on a higher plane can be achieved. Because this viewpoint and technique itself are only conventions, I do not intend to detract from them. On the other hand, the world of tone that I envisage is not the kind of world in which existences can be guaranteed by mathematical thinking. I rather propose a world of tonal existence in which each moment is interred with a vital, self-breathing tonal event.

In A FIELD, I have tried to construct a situation in which tones are given such a mode of being. In order to realize my design, the work is carefully restricted to the use of "tone" itself. It is vital, therefore, that the sounds be performed by persons with rich sensibilities: the tone and its producer should be inseparable. In my composition, "tone" is not thought of quantitatively in the European manner (Tone color can only be perceived as a complex of factors, but even it is broken down, quantified, by the Europeans.).

As a matter of course, I have used a non-traditional manner of notation. It gives strictly directive information concerning playing method and also about the way in which rapport between the players may be attained and developed. Information on the specific characteristics of the tones themselves, however, is quite ambiguous, conveying only the subjective wishes of the composer. Any instruments from the string (violin) family may be used in performance. It should be noted that the indeterminacy used in this composition is not in agreement with John Cage's approach. I always envisage a total structure (or product), and try to transmit this to the performer and listener as accurately as possible.

- Akimichi Takeda
