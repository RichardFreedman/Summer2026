Part Songs—

(a) "Evening"
(Solo: Mr. F. S. WHITEHEAD)
Albt

(b) "Die Post"
(Cornet Solo by Mr. A. HAIGH)
Schafer

The LIEDERTAFEL.

=== Page 7 ===
"It is a beauteous evening, calm and free,
The holy time is quiet as a nun
Breathless with adoration." —Wordsworth.

(a) DAY again his race has run,
    And the tranquill night comes on,
    Soothingly her pinions waving,
    Heated hill and valley laving,
    Spreading from her starry chart.
        Holy peace to every dwelling,
        Holy peace to ev'ry heart.

    All who rest in slumber's arm,
    Void of trouble, void of harm,
    Heavn'ly angels now are guarding,
    Ev'ry danger from them warding,
    Pow'rs of evil must depart—
        Holy peace to every dwelling,
        Holy peace to ev'ry heart.

(b) THRO' forest deep at dead of night,
    The mail is slowly borne;
    While sleep the weary travellers,
    The post-boy winds his horn.

    The forest-ranger's dwelling
    Within the wood is seen,
    The horn a tale is telling—
    What can its echoes mean?

    It's tunes so soft and sweetly flow,
    The winds, in passing by,
    Pause at an open window,
    And mingling with them fly.

    Where sleeps the forest maiden,
    While beams the moon above;
    With love her dreams are laden—
    With melody and love.

    "The horn sounds, and produces a general bustle. . . . As the coach rattles through the village, everyone runs to the window, and you have glances on every side of fresh country faces, and blooming, giggling girls." —Washington Irving.

Part Songs—

(a) "O Sanctissima"

(b) "Warrior's Prayer" Lachner

The LIEDERTAFEL.

(a) Sanctissima! O Piissima!
    Dulcis Virgo, Maria,
    Mater amata Intemerata;
    Ora, ora pro nobis!

(b) Thou art, O Lord, my star by night,
    By day my sun above me;
    Thou art my confidence and might,
    That onward ever move me;
    With Thee o'er deserts do I go,
    And brave in trust no terror know.

    Give me the power my sword to wield,
    And steel my blade to serve me,
    That ere my purpose I shall yield,
    No foe may e'er unnerve me;
    Aid me when I oppose the foe,
    And guide my eye to strike the blow.

    On mountain's height wher'er I stray
    From faltering horse protect me,
    And let Thy angel on my way
    Thro' wood and wild direct me;
    And when upon the trackless sand,
    Be Thou my ever-guiding hand.

    The balmy breeze upon me send,
    To cooling shade convey me,
    E'er head and heart in conflict end,
    And foot and eye betray me;
    And fill me into quiet rest,
    In dreams of home afar caressed.

    Thou art, O Lord, my star by night,
    By day my sun above me.

"Here pause we gentle, for a space;
    And if our tale hath won your grace
    Grant us brief passage, and again
    We will renew the Minstrel strain."
    —Scott.

Special Notice.

One of the Greatest Musical Events of the Year will take place with the presentation early in July next of Mozart's "Requiem," by the Liedertafel, under the baton of the Hon. Conductor, Professor Marshall-Hall. This Immortal work has been in rehearsal for some time past, and every effort will be made to ensure a performance worthy of the best traditions of the Society.

Part II.

"Masters, play here; I will content your pains,—
Something that's brief."
—Othello, Act III., Scene I.

Overture—
"Oberon" Weber
Adagio. Allegro con fuoco.

The ORCHESTRA.

SINCE the creation of Shakespere's "Midsummer Night Dream," no such delightfully picturesque suggestion of the world of sprites, elves and fairies has been given to us as is contained in this romantic Overture. From the mysterious horn call, with which the introduction opens, and the gradual awakening of the drowsy little hamsters of the woodland, to the brilliant and lively theme of the Allegro, and the delightful suggestion of sweet melodies which follow, everything is invested with a profound sympathy with Nature, whose myriad life seems here informed with soul understanding. As we listen to this deeply penetrating poet, the line of demarcation between higher and lower organisms fades away, and their hidden secrets, the unity of life, is disclosed. It is this highly beautiful revelation which causes us to experience such aesthetic pleasure, unmixed with any feeling of incongruity.
—G. W. L. M. H.

Part Song—
"The Image of the Rose" Reichardt
Solo: Miss ELSIE DAVIES.
The LIEDERTAFEL.
WHILE through a valley I was straying,
A rose fresh blooming met my sight,
Such ample store of charm displaying,
My bosom felt unknown delight.

=== Page 12 ===
With fragrant moss around it swelling,
Appeared the gem of lustre mild;
Oh, ne'er from out a fairer dwelling;
The angel face of virtue smiled.
Beautiful form, tarry, oh, tarry with me.

A strange yet pleasing sense came oe'r me,
I felt new life within me bound,
While I beheld the flow'r before me,
Unwonted rapture then I found.
That image fair of heav'nly pleasure,
Upon my heart is deeply traced;
It is my bosom's dearest treasure,
And never can it be o'ersc'd.
Beautiful form, tarry, oh, tarry with me.

When sorrow's clouds are round me low'ring
At once the rose's form appears,
A charm such anguish overpow'ring,
It stills my sighs, it dries my tears.
Oh flow'r that 'mid the darkness springing,
By heav'n's decree upon me shone;
To thee my heart is fondly clinging,
And will not cease till life is gone.
Beautiful form, tarry, oh, tarry with me.

"Ye flowers, . . . . Bright gems of earth, in which,
Perchance, we see
What Eden was—what Paradise may be."
—Anon.

Symphonic Ode—
The Desert
Felicien David

Translated from the French for the Melbourne Liedertafel by Madame Carlotta Tasca.

The LIEDERTAFEL and ORCHESTRA.

"The rosy tints of morning pierce the haze—
The eastern sky illumining with its rays,
From the heav'n above,
The star of day, like a loud hymn of praise,
Fills all the Desert with new light and love.

Part III.
Sleep.
The Rising of the Sun.

The Caravan resumes its progress.

Chorus.
With cheerful heart
On our way let us start,
Toil heeding not, our aim to gain
We roam through the Desert dreary;
Still! on we press nor stay for rest,
Nor stay for rest, though the days are long
And our limbs are weary.
Away we march, ever free and gay,
So pure the air, so bright the day!
We blithely sing as we march along,
Our feet keep time to our rhythmic song.

The Caravan disappears in the distance.

Chorus.
STLLAH! Allah! To Thee I homage render.
Of Thine immensity,
Of Thine eternity,
Image am I, my praise I tender.
Allah!
All praise to Thee; Thy glory is unbounded,
The Sovereign of the world Thou art;
In solitude though I should wander,
Yet Thou art there, in every part.
Allah!

"Sleep dwell upon thine eyes, peace in thy breast."
—Romeo and Juliet. Act II., Scene II.

"A little warmth, a little light
Of loves bestowing, and so, good night."
—Du Maurier.
