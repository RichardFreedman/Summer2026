Overture—
"Coriolan" L. van Beethoven
The ORCHESTRA.

THANKS to Plutarch's stirring "Life" and Shakespeare's yet more stirring drama, everyone can know how that mighty warrior Caius Marcus, surrounded by circumstance delivered Rome repeatedly from the greedy grasp of her foes; and how that, having done so, he was shamefully handled by the ungrateful people he had saved spirited out as they were by a few ignorant and envious mob orators; how that he was banished the state and reviled with nothing vengeance with a vast and invincible army; how that on the eve of the fulfilment of his just wrath his wife and little son and mother sentenced to the hostile camp and after long entreaty prevailed on him to withdraw his troops, conscious as he was that this course was tantamount to signing his own death warrant—all these events subsequently proved.

It is this tragic crisis with which Beethoven has dealt in the present powerful tragic overture, how truthfully will be felt by those who can sympathise with the contending passions which must have torn the warrior's heart during the terrible interludes.

goda sovente pingers de' suoi colori oculti!

quest'efficio dolceissimo signor dell'avvenire, quando ne' cieli il raggio di sua belta veda e tutta me paseca de quel divino error.

L. van Beethoven

Adagio

The ORCHESTRA.

This the first of the great master's symphonic works, will be especially interesting owing to the rarity of its performance and the opportunity it affords us of observing the development of the genius of Beethoven.
Throughout the work the influence of Haydn and Mozart is felt, and it has been said that the Andante un rit. even have been written by a Mozart or a Haydn.
The Third Movement, however, clearly prophesies the scherzo of the later symphonies, as do some of the earlier chamber works of the master.
With the last Movement we are carried back to the graceful time of Haydn, with all its quaintness and light-hearted gaiety.

"The Page Song"
(Romeo and Juliet)
Gounod

Miss CESCA VOLLOGI.

SINCE yesterday I've sought in vain my master.
Oh Capulet, perhaps he yet honours thy house!
I'll sing a stave—so the servants will come
Flocking out to repair yesterday's sad disaster.

Gentle dove wherefore art thou clinging
To a wild vulture's nest?
Soon, we trust, thou wilt be a-winging
To a far dearer rest.

For the vulture loves fight and faray,
And to tear and mangle his quarry
Hath beak both sharp and strong.
Unlike thine, soft and true and slender.
Oh may thine bend to lips more tender,
With kisses warm and long.

See you guard her safely,
They that live will know
For your dove may flutter
From her cage and go.

Now, it happ'd, that a ringdove flying
From her woodland so green.
To that eyrie came one eve sighing
For her young love, I ween.
O'er a banquet of green they'd mangled
In the nest the vulture's brood wrangled.
Hark! rose their cry afar,
But the dove, far away was hearing
Love's own voice in its tones endearing
Beneath the evening star.

See you guard her safely,
They that live will know
For your dove may flutter
From her cage and go.

Madrigal—
"Down in a Flow'ry Vale"
Constantine Festa (1541)

LIEDERTAFEL and ASSOCIATED LADIES' CHOIR.

DOWN in a flow'ry vale, all on a summer morning
Phillis I spied, fair nature's self adorning:
Swiftly on wings of love, I flew to meet her,
Coldly she welcomed me when I did greet her.

I warbled thus my ditty:
O shepherdess have pity,
And hear a faithful lover, his passion true discover.
Ah! why art thou to me so cruel?
Then straight replied my jewel—
If gold thou hast, fond youth, 'twill speed thy suiting,
But if thy purse be empty, come not to me a wooing.

Soon as I careless strayed, fond youth, with eyes averted,
Phillis I met, by all the swains deserted;
Swift she (tho' late so coy) then flew to meet me,
My back I turn'd, all deaf to her entreaty;
She warbled thus her ditty:
O shepherd, now have pity,
And to your faithful lover your passion true discover
Then did I, cold and naughty, view her,
And thus replied unto her—
The love that's won by gold will prove undoing.
So since my purse is empty, I go no more a wooing.
