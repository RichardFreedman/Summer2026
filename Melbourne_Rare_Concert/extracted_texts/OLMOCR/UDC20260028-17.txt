PART I

Cantata—The Waterlily
Miss Minnie Waugh and Liedertafel
GOETZ

In evening stillness lies the lake,
    And mirrors the waning glow of the west,
Their thirst come timorous deer to slake,
    And swans fly, encircling its tranquil breast.

The fisher boy unfastens his boat,
    The plash of his oar thro' the silence steals;
In Fancy's realm does he appear to float,
    He knows not why in his heart thus he feels.

Around him whisper the darkling waves,
    And sing in their motion, far and wide,
The water a dim wooded margin laves,
    And the spectre-like mountains tower in pride.

The cooling breath of the evening air,
    It plays thro' his locks all golden and bright,
A fragrance comes from the flow'rets fair,
    As low they are bending for sleep at night.

=== Page 8 ===
But the waterlily is yet awake,
    Though nodding in slumb'rous mood is she;
She bends unto him from her native lake,
    Tho' evening invites her at rest to be.

His dream is dispelled, and he quits his oar;
    His face is on fire with longing confessed;
He stoops from the boat, the spot to explore,
    He plucks off a lily, thus decking his breast.

But hark! with fury the forest shakes,
    The lake uplifts its roaring on high,
And sudden over a mountain breaks
    The ruddy full moon, so wrathful of eye.

And the lake exclaims, with heart deeply moved,
    "Why trouble the children within their home?"
And the forest complains, "These long have I loved,
    Why force these delightful sisters to roam?"

But the red-glowing moon then fiercely cries,
    "How darest thou to address my bride?"
And in terror the boy with tears homeward flies,
    That there with his mother, his grief he may hide.

    "O mother, the roar of the lake I heard,
    Against me the forest with fury shook,
Before me, in anger, the full moon appeared,
    O mother, I but a lily took!"

(b) Wanderer's Song
    Schumann-Plumpton

Liedertafel

(a) Dioscuri! shining twin stars,
    Ye who light us o'er the ocean;
    Mighty helpers, guide us safely,
    Still the waves to gentle motion.

    Tho' with firm hearts, fear unknowing,
        Undismayed with storms contending.
    We would see your mild rays beaming,
        Hope and courage to us lending.

    This strong oar that bears us onward,
        O'er the waves your rule obeying.
    At your shrine we then will offer,
        At your altar homage paying.

(b) Again fill your glasses with bright sparkling wine,
    Once more will I pledge ye, dear comrades of mine!
    Farewell, hoary mountains and dear native home,
    Farewell! to a far distant land I must roam.

    The bright sun above us at rest does not stand,
    His course he fulfils over ocean and land:
    The billows disperse and recede from the shore,
    The storm rushes onward its fury to pour.

    With swift-speeding pinion the bird flies away,
    Still singing and soaring it pours out its lay.
    Thus youth's glowing visions will urge to the race,
    Thus earth wanders on thro' regions of space.

    The birds of his own land the wanderer greet,
    Its flow'r's meet his eye with their odour so sweet;
    They bring to his heart thoughts of home and of love,
    The meadow beneath and the blue sky above.

=== Page 10 ===
He dreams that once more he his fatherland sees,
And sweet scents of home life come borne on the breeze;
Thus love e'er will follow the lov'd one afar,
And shine through the distance his one guiding star.

(La Damnation de Faust) Berlioz
Liedertafel
Soldiers.
FORTRESSES defended with strong ramparts high,
Fair maidens who daringly love's power defy,
Of conquest we're certain soon as we take the field.
We reck not of danger, to us they must yield.
At the sound of the trumpet we soldiers
To revel or battle at once march away.

Students.
Jam nox stellata vel anima pandit,
Nunc bibendum et amandum est,
Vita brevis fugax que voluptas,
Gaudeamus igitur.

Nobis sub ridente luna.
Per urben quaerentes puellas eamus,
Ut cras fortunati Cæsares dicamus,
Veni, vidi, vici, gaudeamus igitur.

PART II

Grand Double Choruses from Oedipus at Colonus

Music by Felix Mendelssohn-Bartholdy

Liedertafel

Theseus, followed by the citizens of Athens, again appears, and, learning what Creon has done, sends for soldiers and renews his promises of protection to Oedipus, declaring that his daughters shall be restored to him, and that Creon shall not be allowed to bring such a disgrace upon Athens. The Chorus describes the contest, and invokes the aid of the Gods in favour of Theseus and his followers.

Ah, were I on yonder plain,
    Where now our undaunted sons
        Stand arm'd for the hostile fray;
There, where the shrine of Phoebus gleams,
        And torches are burning;
Where mortals bow down to the awe-inspiring pair,
    Whose hallow'd rites are mystic deeds,
        Wrought by Eumolpidan priests,
    And never breath'd to mortal ear.
        There, glowing with ardour,
            Panting to rush against the foe,
                See, see all our legions,
        With Theseus they wave their swords, and cry,
            Rescue the sisters!

=== Page 12 ===
Or westward perchance they rush,
Where snow-cover'd mountains rise
    O'er Œa's enamell'd meads;
There dancing cars and prancing steeds
    Will clash in the onset!
There on the foe soon will impending vengeance fall,
    For Theseus leads our valiant bands:
    See their arms and armour gleaming
Bright as the trappings that grace their steeds!
    Now proudly exulting.
They, who honour the deeds of sage Minerva,
And Rhea's great son, earth-encircling ocean's king,
    Press to the battle!

    Do they await, pursue or fight?
        My heart thrills with hope
That they will soon rescue the captive maid,
Who hath endur'd, and still endures her kindred's woe.
    This day, Jove will restore her,
    Crown your deeds, heroes, with glory!
    Like a dove high soaring,
    Sped along by the storm-wind!
    I, from the verge of a cloud,
    Would look down on the contest,
And see my country's cause triumphant!

    Dread Pow'r, that fillest heav'n's high throne,
    O all-seeing Jove, defend our great ruler!
        On him the victor's palm bestow!
        With verdant laurels deck his brow!
            We call on thee, Pallas Athene!
            Mighty hunter, Phœbus, hear us!
And thy sister, fleet in chase, whose dart unerring
    Pierceth the far distant hart:
    O come, defend our guardians!
    We pray ye, aid our native heroes!

The Athenians are successful, and Theseus returns with Antigone and Ismene. Œdipus expresses his gratitude to the King, who announces the arrival of Polynices, seeking an interview with his father. The Chorus describes the sorrows of life, and the blessing of death when old age has come.

=== Page 13 ===
II

When the health and the strength are gone.
He that still would be lingering here,
Borne down by a load of care,
Is, I consider, devoid of wisdom.
Old age bringeth disease and pain,
Grief, anguish, and regret remain.
For youth's bloom and its fleeting joys
Never, never return again.
Life's varied journeys lead to death!
Smooth or rugged, all its pathways,
Even its wishes, end in Hades.
Love hymeneal, revels, dances, strains of music
    Cease, when the Foe appeareth!

Better not to exist;
Or, if born, in early childhood to die,
And escape all the cares and pains.
They, living longest, endure the longest.
Youth and Folly and Sorrow roam
Hand in hand to their destined home:
For none liveth devoid of care;
None escapeth what all must bear.
Envy, Sedition, Murder, Strife,
Fear and rage hover around us:
And, in conclusion, feeble, helpless,
Old and friendless, all these ills together meeting
    Crush their devoted victim.

On man, poor mourner, all these evils fall,
Fall as billows rushing beat the north strand,
Dashing onward, urged by the blast of winter.
Thus, then, storms of affliction, never-ending, assail us.
    Thus lowering sorrows around us gather;
    Whether from Helios' western bound,
        Or from his eastern gate,
        Or where its rays beam brightest,
        Or down from the home of Arcturus.

=== Page 14 ===
The Chorus sing the praises of Colonos,

Thou comest here to the land, O friend,
Fam'd for fleet-footed steeds and blooming meadows;
Thou standest now in Colonos' grove,
    Where the voices of nightingales
    Resound, floating in dulcet strains
        Thro' their temples of verdure;
    Where the thick-woven ivy clings,
    Spreading over the sacred aisles;
Where, in security, ripens the fruitful vine,
    Unscath'd by beam or stormwind.
And here roameth the bounteous god,
Gay and good Dionysos, ever greeted in lays,
    Sung by the nymphs who rear'd him.

Fair and fragrant at dawn of day,
See the beautiful heaven-bedewed Narcissus,
The flow'r that shone in the goddesses' crowns,
    Worn by them in the days of old.
See the Crocus array'd in gold;
    Hear the slumberless waters,
While they glide in Cephisus' tide,
Still meandering day by day
Over the plains, bearing streams ever pure along,
    Enriching all the bowers
Embosomed in the favour'd land;
Ever loved by the tuneful choir of Muses,
    And Cythera, whose zone is golden.

Here, too, bloometh a tree
Crowned with a fruit not found in Asia;
Nor on Dorian plains doth it appear,
Gracing the island where the Pelops
Held sway! yet here it freely springeth;
    Rever'd by friends, and fear'd by foes,
    In native splendour it blossoms for Athens.
Behold, ours is the rich oil-dropping olive!
    Aged and youthful, noble and peasant,
Never wantonly harm this holy tree.
    Guarded and cherished, it stands secure,
    Ever heeded by Morian Jove,
        Lov'd and watch'd by Minerva.

=== Page 15 ===
Grateful, let me extol
Heaven's richest gifts deigned to my country;
Presented by the Pow'r making our State
Fam'd for its wealth, honour, and glory:
    Our land and sea steeds, rein and rudder-guided;
    O Chronos' son, by them we stand pre-eminent,
        Mighty god Poseidon,
Thou didst give us the rein form'd for the courser;
Guided thus, or curbed, he yields obedience.
Through the billows we dash, stemming the deep,
    Speeded along by the oar and helm,
    Bounding on with the dancing nymphs—
        Nereus' hundred-footed daughters.

Part Song—Night in the Forest
Liedertafel
    Ever sweet art thou, O Night!
        In the forest sweeter far.
    Where thy starry eyes gleam bright,
        Where thy soft enchantments are!
    Where the leaves, like fairy goblets,
        Overflow with dewy wine,
    And the moon's unclouded radiance
        Fills each glade with light divine.

=== Page 16 ===
Murmuring breezes sing of thy pleasures,
Silvery moonbeams weave thee thy tresses;
All whom thy lips have silently press'd
Closing their eyes, sink sweetly to rest;

But who would sleep on night so clear?
Arise! the horn rings far and near,
And wakens with its gladsome sound
Each slumb'rer in the groves around.
    Wake! wake! wake!

It echoes the woods and the mountain tops o'er,
The birds, all awaking, think night is no more;
The wandering deer in affright flee away,
They fancy it heralds the dawn of the day;
The lords of the forest shout loud in their might,
The fountain awakes with a song of delight!

Then sing as we wander,
    "The woods are the home of the night!"
And echo answers yonder,
    "The woods, thy home, O Night!"
Yes, here within thy forest home,
    We'll greet thee, lovely, lovely Night.
Where smiling from thy heav'nly dome,
    Thou reign'st in all thy might!

Part Song—A Capstan Chorus
Liedertafel
SMART

ROLLING along, bowling along, over the sea we go,
    And heave our anchor singing our song,
With a yeo! ho! cheery men, yeo!
    The wind and the waves may beat us about,
And rocks' lie waiting below,
    But our yards they are trim, and our timbers are stout,
So yeo! ho! cheery men, yeo!

Monstrous and terrible roars the old sea,
    When winds make his wild rage grow;
Grim death lurks then in his heart "for we,"
    But yeo! ho! cheery men, yeo!

For well are we nurst on his broad boon breast,
    When his rage shall overblow,
Fed full of the bold free life which is best,
    Yeo! cheery men, yeo! ho!

How he swings him along 'neath his ocean of air,
    In his great heart's careless flow;
How we win his love when his wrath we dare,
    Sing yeo! ho! cheery men, yeo!

GOD SAVE THE QUEEN

Memoranda

Subscribers and friends are reminded that the Subscription to this Society has been materially reduced, and the entrance fee abolished.

This is the third Concert of the Twenty-sixth Season. Three are given to ladies and gentlemen, and two to gentlemen only.

Intending subscribers may join Class A, £1 1s.; Class B, £2 2s.; Class C, £1 11s. 6d.

Class A.—Two tickets for each Concert to ladies and gentlemen, and one for each Concert to gentlemen only—8 TICKETS.

Class B.—Five tickets for each Concert to ladies and gentlemen, and two for each Concert to gentlemen only—19 TICKETS.

Class C.—Three tickets for each Concert to ladies and gentlemen, and one for each Concert to gentlemen only—11 TICKETS.

Life Subscribers.—The sum of Ten Guineas will secure for life the privileges of Class A.

Subscribers joining during the year are only charged pro rata.

Subscribers' tickets are transferable by endorsement.

Extra tickets, which are only sold to Subscribers, are 4s. for Concerts to ladies and gentlemen, and 2s. 6d. for Concerts to gentlemen only.

The Hon. Secretary will be glad to receive nominations, from ladies and gentlemen desiring to become Subscribers.
