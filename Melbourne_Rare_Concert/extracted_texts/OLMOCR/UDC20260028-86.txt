FROM KEMBLE TO DONALD DUCK
A HUNDRED and thirty years ago The Times was engaged in open warfare with the theatres. The paper had adopted the novel practice of giving its readers genuine dramatic criticism. The theatrical managers of the day preferred the old system (not entirely extinct even now) of "puffing and plenty of tickets."
The Times won its fight, and it has jealously guarded ever since the tradition then established. Its treatment of the theatre is still anonymous, and independent alike of theatrical cliques and of "dining table amenities."
To-day the variety and scope of The Times Entertainments Page would astonish both the critic and the play-goer of a bygone age. Indeed, they may well surprise the modern reader who is not fully familiar with them.
The function of the page is to provide a complete guide for the modern play- or film- goer, as well as for the lover of music and the other arts. Every new play in London is reviewed at length on the morning after its first performance. Twice a week an account of forthcoming productions is given. But that is not all. The theatres of Paris and New York are regularly noticed, and special articles, which appear at frequent intervals, keep the interested reader in touch with the contemporary theatre throughout the world.
It is true that The Times has not yet found space for gossip about film stars. But the great figures of the cinema are discussed in relation to their art. Charlie Chaplin and Donald Duck have both been the subject of critical appreciation.
Music, too—opera, ballet, concerts and recitals, including important events in the provinces and abroad—is ably discussed in this daily Entertainments Page, and Art Exhibitions of all kinds are treated with the same authority.

SCENES FROM THE SONG OF HIAWATHA

In performance a few words have been altered to bring the narrative into the present tense. The story of this Opera forms a part of Longfellow's "Song of Hiawatha." It is divided into three Acts: "Hiawatha's Wedding Feast," "The Death of Minnehaha," and "Hiawatha's Departure."

To understand the story it is necessary to know that it had been revealed to the Indian tribes by Gitche Manitou, the Great Spirit and Creator of all living things, that he would send to them from the land where there is no sin and no darkness, and where all is bathed in the glory of light and love, a great prophet who would teach them to adore the beauty of peace and who, after suffering with them the sorrows and privations of mankind, would lead them into the golden dawn of a new world.

In the fulness of time, Hiawatha was born, and became a great prophet, teacher and statesman. Under his influence five adjacent tribes buried the hatchet and welded themselves together as a powerful confederation which became allied to the British settlers and fought beside them against the Hurons and their allies, the French. Thus, Hiawatha played a considerable part in the history of the formation of the British Empire, in that he assisted to a great extent in the acquisition of Canada.

I.—HIAWATHA'S WEDDING FEAST

Hiawatha was a man as other men, and wooed and won sweet Laughing Water, Minnehaha from the land of the Dacotahs. The Opera opens with the festivities at their marriage, and gay are the guests as they offer their presents and receive the generous hospitality of their host. Quip and jest pass round, and Chibibabos, the singer of sweet songs, sounds the keynote of passionate love in the ears of his entranced listeners. Dance follows song, Pau-Puk-Keewis leaps and twines with the grace of a barbaric savage. The Medicine Man brews a Peyote plant potion, of which the guests partake, and through its magic visualize a dream ballet of childhood days. Then follows a thrilling totem dance, in which all the tribe take part, and give vent to their feeling in the hiss of the serpent, the bark of the fox, the shriek of the eagle or the bellow of the buffalo, according to the nature of their totem.

The Marriage Feast is over. Happy and joyous, the guests depart to their wigwams, and alone in the moonlight are Hiawatha and Minnehaha.

II.—THE DEATH OF MINNEHAHA

Summer is over, and Winter grips valley and mountain in its icy clutch. Fever and Famine stalked abroad. Clinging to each other for warmth, the stricken Indians cower and shiver beneath their blankets. And what of the lovely Minnehaha—"Minnehaha, Laughing Water, Loveliest of Dacotah women"? In the absence of Hiawatha—who has gone to seek vainly for food for his people, succour for his bride—she is sick unto death, and dread phantoms bend over her couch. Finally, with a wild cry of "Hiawatha! Hiawatha!" she dies in delirium. "Far away amid the forest, miles away among the mountains" the wanderer hears that cry. Homeward hurries Hiawatha. Passionately, despairingly, he flings himself beside the dead body of his beloved, and the old nurse Nokomis sobs out her anguish. The sorrowing Indians moan and wail forth their sorrow, and over the mountains falls the snow.

There is a pause to show the passage of seven days, and then the body of Minnehaha is borne away in stately procession, clothed in her richest garments, wrapped in her robes of ermine, ready for her journey to the Islands of the Blessed.

Nature is eternally young. The Third Act opens with a Ballet of Spring, symbolizing life bursting forth throughout a rejuvenated world. Then Iago, the boaster, tells of how he had seen 100 warriors coming over a great water—"bitter so that none could drink it"—in a "great canoe with pinions." He is mocked at by the Indians. "Kaw?" they say, "what lies you tell us!" But Hiawatha declares that Iago speaks truth. The strangers are the coming White Race—traders, settlers and missionaries—bringing to the depths of the virgin forest civilization and the Faith and Hope of Christianity.

And presently this prophecy is fulfilled. The Black-Robe Chief, with the cross upon his bosom, accompanied by his guides and companions, lands on the sandy margin of the lake—bearing a message of Peace and Goodwill on earth.

Hiawatha's mission is at an end. Bidding his people cultivate friendship and unity, he steps into his magic canoe, and as it floats down the stream, the whole tribe raise their arms and their voices in final farewell. As their voices sound and swell and triumph, the vast crowd, the camp, valleys, trees and distant mountain peaks are mantled in solemn light, and in a blaze of sound and colour, Hiawatha westward passes away into the golden sunset. So ends the "Song of Hiawatha."

In the purple mists of evening,
