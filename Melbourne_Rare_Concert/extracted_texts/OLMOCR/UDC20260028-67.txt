Ballet Madrigals—

(a) My Bonny Lass, She Smileth
(b) Now is the Month of Maying

ROYAL VICTORIAN LIEDERTAFEL

(a)
My bonny lass, she smileth,
When she my heart beguileth,
Fa, la, la!
Smiles less, dear love, therefore,
And you shall love me more,
Fa, la, la!
When she her bright eye turneth,
O, how my heart it burneth,
Fa, la, la!
Dear love, call in their light,
Or else you burn me quite.
Fa, la, la!

(b)
Now is the month of Maying,
When merry lads are playing,
Fa, la, la.
Each with his bonny lass,
A-dancing on the grass,
Fa, la, la.
The spring, clad all in gladness,
Doth laugh at winter's sadness,
And to the bagpipes' sound
The nymphs tread out the ground,
Fie, then, why sit we musing?
Youth's sweer delight refusing?
Say, dainty nymphs, and speak,
Shall we play barley-break?

These famous Madrigals are, of course, well known to mixed-voice choirs, but are rarely sung by men's choirs.
We are indebted to Dr. Davison, the Conductor of the Harvard University Glee Club, for arranging them for male voices.

(a)
Summer is a-coming in,
Loud now sing cuckoo,
Groweth seed and bloweth mead,
And spring the woods anew.
Ewe now bleateth after lamb,
Loweth after calf the cow,
Bullock starteth, buck now darteth,
Merry sing cuckoo.

Cuckoo, cuckoo, well singest thou, cuckoo,
Nor cease thou never now.

(b)
Owre Kynge went forth to normandie,
with grace and myght of chyvalrie
ther God for him wrought mervulously
wher fore englande may calle and cry
Deo gracias.

* * * *
Than went owre Kynge with alle his oste
thorwe fraunce for alle the frenshe hoste
he spared no drede of lest ne moste
tyl he come to agincourt coste
Deo gracias.

* * * *
Ther dukys and erlys lorde and barone
wer take and slayne and that wel sone
and summe wer ladde unto Lundone
with joye and merthe and grete renone
Deo gracias.
Now gracius God he save owre Kynge
his peple and alle his wel wyllynge
gef him gode lyf and gode endyng
that we with merth mowe savely synge
Deo Gracias.

(c)
Pastymye with good companye
I love, and schall untyl I dye
Gruche who lust but none denye,
So God be plesyd thus leve wyll I.
For my pastance, hunt, syng, and daunce;
My hart is sett,
At goodly sport
For my comfort,
Who schal me let?

Companye with honeste
Is vertu, vices to fle.
Companye is good or ill, but evry man hath
hys fre wyll.
The best ensen.
The worst eschew.
My mynde schal be

Folk Songs—

(a) Bushes and Briars (Essex) Arr. Vaughan Williams
(b) Ca' Hawkie Thro' the Watter (Northumberland) Arr. W. G. Whittaker
(Presented by Mr. A. Valentine Soul)
(c) The British Grenadiers (17th Century) Arr. Percy Fletcher

ROYAL VICTORIAN LIEDERTAFEL

(a)
Through bushes and through briars
I lately took my way,
All for to hear the small birds sing
And the lambs to skip and play,
I overheard my own true love,
Her voice it was so clear:
"Long time I have been waiting
For the coming of my dear.
Sometimes I am uneasy and troubled in my mind,
Sometimes I think I'll go to my love,
And tell to him my mind,
And if I should go to my love,
My love, he will say nay;
If I show to him my boldness,
He'll ne'er love me again.
If I show my boldness he'll ne'er love me again."

(b)
Ca' Hawkie, through the watter,
Hawkie is a sweir beast,
And Hawkie winna wade the watter,
Hawkie is a bonnie coo!
Tho' she's loath to wade the watter,
While she waits the work'll stand,
So ca' Hawkie through the watter,
Hawkie winna wade the watter,
Hawkie is a prettier coo!
All the children do adore her,
For she gives them all the milk,
There is none they prize before her!
Girls, be not too nice or coy,
If you swear a man to marry;
Ne'er say nay, but quickly comply,
As 'tis hazardous to tarry.
Now, young maids, my counsel take,
Since that it can be no better:
Cast off baith your hose and shoon,
And safely drive her through the watter.

GLOSSARY FOR CA' HAWKIE.
Ca—Call.
Watter—Water.
Sweir—Unwilling.
Baith—Both.

Shoon—Shoes.
Winna—Will not.
Coo—Cow.

(c)
Some talk of Alexander, and some of Hercules;
Of Hector and Lysander, and such great names as these;
But of all the world's brave heroes there's none that can compare
With a tow row row row row row row
To the British Grenadiers.

Those heroes of antiquity ne'er saw a cannon ball,
Or knew the force of powder to slay their foes withal;
But our brave boys do know it, and banish all their fears;
Sing tow row row row row row row,
For the British Grenadiers.

When e'er we are commanded to storm the palisades,
Our leaders march with fuses, and we with hand grenades;
We throw them from the glacis about the enemies' ears;
Sing tow row row row row row row
For the British Grenadiers.

And when the siege is over, we to the town repair,
The townsman cry, "Hurra, boys! here comes a Grenadier."
Here come the Grenadiers, my boys, who know no doubts or fears,
Then sing tow row row row row row
For the British Grenadiers.

Then let us fill a bumper and drink a health to those
Who carry caps and pouches and wear the loup'd clothes;
May they and their commanders live happy all their years,
With a tow row row row row row
For the British Grenadiers.

PART II.

Let brother to brother good fellowship proffer:
    Apollo invites us, come bow at his shrine.
The glee and the catch are the incense we offer,
    The bond that unites us is music divine.

Chorus, Introduction to Act I., "Ernani"—
    The Bandit
        Verdi

ROYAL VICTORIAN LIEDERTAFEL

All welcome the bowl!
A full flowing soul,
    Unfathomed by care!
What boots it the bandit,
If hunted and branded,
    So wine be his share!

    To play, sirs!
Treasure is merely a pleasure
    That flies soon as come!
And play hath a duty,
It charmeth when beauty
    Inconstant may roam.

Thrice welcome the bowl!
Whose glorious soul
    Doth bleed at our call!
The bowl, the bowl,
Whose glorious soul, sirs,
    Doth bleed at our call.

Ernani, still pensive?
    Quick thought grows apprehensive
        To view his cheek so blanch'd
    Where life's tide hath motion,
On Death's shoreless ocean
    Our one bark is launched.

    To give, then thy willing
Immediate fulfilling,
    Ere will'd we prepare.
The hawk's speed 'gan tarries
In hitting her quarries,
    If ours ye compare.

    Come, welcome the bowl,
Whose glorious soul
    Can drown all thy care!

LESLIE McCALLUM. Baritone and Flautist.

Part Song—
I May No Longer Dream Armstrong Gibbs
(Presented by Mr. E. Casper)

ROYAL VICTORIAN LIEDERTAFEL

I may no longer dream
Here by the solitary tulip-tree
That Leirion waits for me;
Nor in the cool
Shade of the sombre cypress look to see
Narcissus, bowed above the marble rim,
Blurring with crystal tears his image in the pool.

I may no more esp'y
By yonder mouldering plinth,
Lord Eros, veiled, with lanthorn lifted high,
Guiding his virgin train,
Hippolytus, Adonis, Hyacinth,
Hylas and Agathon—
Lilies new-blown and clear of earthly stain—
Home to the unguessed heights of his serene domain.
Ah me! ah me!
Must I go mourn for earth-born phantoms fled,
And those Uranian summers lived in vain?
Nay, fond, inveterate lover!

Now, now at last lift up a joyful head!
Thy perilous day is done,
The blessed night draws on—
Love is no dream, as thou wilt prove tomorrow,
When thou shalt wake to find Him standing at thy side.
See where the quiet garden waits for thee!
The sheltering boughs their evening balm have shed;
Dear memories, undefiled;
Gather like dew; closer the shadows creep.
Beneath thy chosen Leirion-haunted tree
Make thou thy lonely bed;
Lie down and rest, by no false dreams beguiled;
With folded hands pray God thy soul to keep,
Even as a little child,
Weary, and very glad because of coming sleep.

—Paul England.

"Young Dietrich"—Words after the German of Felix Dahn.
Defeated and slain with half of his host,
Was Theodemerus, King of the Goths.
The Huns were shouting with fiendish mirth,
The vultures hastened down to earth:
The moon was bright, the wind was cold,
The wolves were howling in distant wold.
Three men were riding through heather and field,
Their helmets battered, and broken their shield.
The first one bore in his saddle sling
The broken spear of his murdered King;
The second, he bore the King's crown, Oh, woe!
Split straight in two, by the axe of a foe.
The third, he hid in faithful arm
A secret veiled in his mantle warm,
A veiled secret!
And when they the river Iser espied,
The first one rested his horse and cried:
"Save a battered crown and a broken spear,
Of the Gothic Kingdom is naught to fear"!

And the second, he said, "In those waters deep,
Let us bury our treasure for ever to sleep,
Then follow ourselves, by a leap from the sand:
Why tarry, Master Hildebrand?"
"Ye carry the King's own crown and spear:
My faithful companions, far more have I here."

Thus saying, he opened his cloak so warm;
"I carry the hope of the Goths in my arm.
Ye saved crown and spear, 'twas bravely done;
I—I have rescued the King's own son!
Awake now, my boy, see how homage I bring.
I greet thee Young Dietrich, the Goths' new King."
