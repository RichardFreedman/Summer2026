THE Musical Society of Victoria

ANNUAL REPORT
FOR YEAR 1907-8.

THE Council have much pleasure in presenting their Annual Report and Balance-Sheet for 1907-8. Perusal of the transactions of the Society, both on the artistic and financial sides, will show that the year has been one of the most successful yet recorded. The programmes have not only maintained their high-class character, but have also been up-to-date as regards modern music. Owing to the large increase in the attendance the question of a larger hall will have to be considered in the near future.

NEW MEMBERS.—During the year thirty-nine new members were elected.

EXAMINATIONS.—The Examinations conducted by the Society are without doubt a distinct advantage to the community. In studying for these Examinations, students have an aim and purpose. They go into detail more minutely, their attention is more concentrated, and both teacher and pupil know that they cannot ensure success unless they are thoroughly conscientious, determined and whole-souled in their work. The Council would therefore strongly urge all members to prepare students for the Society's Examinations.

The Julius Siede Prize (Preparatory section) was won by Ethel L. Harder and Jean E. Spence (equal) at the November Examination, 1907; and by Foster Nutting, at the May Examination, 1908.

The Special Prize for highest aggregate, with credit, in the Elementary section, at the November Examination, 1907, was awarded to Clarice E. Freeman. The Bronze Medal, for highest aggregate, with credit, in the Intermediate section, at the November Examination, 1907, to Daisy Clarice Purvis (Piano) and Gertrude Carruthers (Violin) equal; and at the May Examination, 1908, to Margaret E. Inglis, Kyneton.

GENERAL MEETINGS.—Ten general meetings and musical evenings have been held, the programme at the June meeting,

=== Page 5 ===
1907, consisting of items by candidates who had gained the highest marks in their respective sections at the previous Examination. The At Home, which was held at Glen's, on February 15th, 1908, was well attended, and a very pleasant evening was spent.

The BALANCE-SHEET shows the Society's finances are in a very satisfactory condition, after paying all expenses the credit balance standing at £228 5s. 4d.

THE LIBRARY.—The additions to the Library during the year were ten operas, in vocal score, presented by Mr. E. H. Hanstein. "Counterpoint, Canon and Fugue," Ousley; "Harmony," Ousley; "Counterpoint" 2 vols., Chernubini, presented by Mr. J. H. Hammond. "The Art of Singing," Henderson; "Quintet, Op. 77," (strings) Dvorak.

COUNCIL MEETINGS.—Fifteen Council Meetings were held, the attendances being as follows: W. H. Heathcote 15, G. Peake 14, H. Curtis 14, S. Lamble 14, Gregor Wood 12, W. Hunter 12, T. J. Lamble 12, M. Greer 12, G. H. Davies 10, A. Zelman 8, E. H. Hanstein 6 (resigned Nov. 1907), H. J. Inge 4, (elected Feb. 1908).

During the year Mr. Gregor Wood and Mr. E. H. Hanstein resigned the offices of Vice-President and Treasurer respectively. The vacancies were filled by the appointment of Mr. Inge as Vice-President and Mr. Gregor Wood as Hon. Treasurer.

The thanks of the Society are due to Messrs. W. H. Glen and Co. for the gratuitous use of their premises as the office of the Society, and to Mr. F. L. Wilson, F.C.P.A., for professional services as auditor.

OBITUARY.—Mr. J. C. W. Nicholson, of the well-known music firm, an honorary member of the Society, passed away in September, 1907. Although living in Sydney, Mr. Nicholson was noted for his liberal gifts to Musical Societies of Melbourne, and his unfailing kindness to musicians generally. It is to be hoped that his generous spirit and practical interest in this and other Musical Societies of Melbourne will inspire others in like position to emulate his example.

To the incoming Council, and to all members of the M.S.V., we would seriously urge that vigilant attention be given to the administration of the Registration of Teachers Act.

GEORGE PEAKE, PRESIDENT.
W. H. HEATHCOTE, SECRETARY.

PRESIDENT'S ADDRESS,
at the Annual Meeting, 20th June, 1908.

LADIES AND GENTLEMEN,--

At the Annual Meeting it is the President's privilege to inflict you with an address, and this evening I desire to speak briefly on the aim and objects of this Society. I have selected this subject as there is evidence that some of the members have no clear idea of the advantage of belonging to it. Unless we have a very real and sincere conviction that the objects of the Society are worthy of our membership, we certainly shall not be able to maintain that energy and enthusiasm of the immediate past, which have done so much to increase our list of members, and strengthen our individual attachment.

In the April issue of The Musical Times, we learn that a new organisation has just been formed, under the title of the Musical League, with Sir Edward Elgar as President, and Sir A. C. Mackenzie, H. J. Wood, Granville Bantock, Percy Pitt, F. Delius, Dr. Brodsky and Dr. McNaught as Members of Committee. Its objects are:

I. To foster the cause of music in England, and to promote the development of musical life and culture throughout the country.
II. By holding an Annual Festival, introducing new compositions, and also older works whose merits entitle them to more frequent performance.
III. By affording opportunity for exchange of ideas upon questions of interest to musicians.
IV. By watching every proposed legislation that may affect them.
V. By taking steps, when necessary, to promote or prevent such legislation.
VI. To assist necessitous musicians of merit in cases of sickness or undeserved misfortune.

I have quoted the above because with the exception of the Annual Festival those objects are identical with the objects of the Musical Society of Victoria. Reference to past transactions will show that the Society has endeavoured to encourage local composers, with the result that original string quartets, a piano and string trio, violin solos and songs, by various local composers, have been introduced from time to time in the Society's programmes. The Society also offered prizes for the best string quartet, part-song or glee, and I am sure I voice the opinion of the whole Society when I say they would be delighted to hear meritorious works by Australian composers more frequently at our meetings.

=== Page 7 ===
In introducing other new works, and "older works whose merits entitle them to more frequent performance," this Society has a splendid record, and one that any Society may well be proud of. Let it be remembered that for many years there would have been no opportunity of hearing the so-called chamber classics but for the existence of the M.S.V. We do not forget the efforts of private enterprise in the sixties, the popular concerts of the late Mr. T. H. Guenett, or those splendid quartet concerts of the last two years given by the Melbourne String Quartet, but in the long interregnum between these efforts, and throughout the whole period, the M.S.V. kept the flag flying, largely by the untiring and unselfish work of its then members, who, without fee or reward, gave us month after month and year after year of their best.

As regards watching proposed legislation that may affect the profession, it may be pointed out that the M.S.V. were mainly instrumental in securing for the individual teacher's pupils the favored treatment as regards train fares, which the Railway Department had given to the pupils of colleges and corporate bodies only. The Society also initiated the movement whereby the whole profession received some recognition of their work by the Registration Board, and here let me say that unless members of the musical profession are content to be placed at the mercy of one set of individuals, one school of thought, and to be deprived of that freedom which is the very life blood of artistic existence and of skilful teaching, they will require to give vigilant attention to the administration of the Registration of Teachers' Act. I may here also express my opinion that in view of the enormous sums by which distinguished visiting artists reduce the spending power of the community, they ought not to demand full price from the members of the musical profession for admission to their concerts. It is reported that the Clara Butt tour realised £51,000, and Madame Melba's few concerts £10,000, yet the musical profession, who, by their teaching, practically create the constituency which these visitors exploit, receive no consideration or concession when attending the concerts.

Another object of the Society is "to assist necessitous musicians of merit in cases of sickness or undeserved misfortune," for which purpose a substantial Benevolent Fund exists. The foregoing facts are sufficient to show that the M.S.V. has anticipated many of the objects for which Sir Edward Elgar, Sir A. C. Mackenzie and others in England now consider it necessary to form a Musical League.

So much for the objects of the Society. Now for the practical advantages offered to members. In the past we have had members resign, not because they could not afford to pay the subscription, but because they could not see that they obtained

=== Page 8 ===
any advantage in belonging to the Society. I have already named some of the advantages which the Society has secured, not alone for its members, but for the whole profession, and have also indicated that in the future the profession will need a strong Society to protect it from legislative injustice, unfair competition, and iniquitous taxation. But apart from these, members who can see no advantage in belonging to such a Society must be ignorant of the influence of environment. Musical environment is absolutely necessary to the development of a musical mind. In a Society like the M.S.V., consisting of professional musicians, members have an environment which the public can never supply. The members encourage performers to produce the highest and purest forms of musical art, but not so the general public. A well-known public singer was once privately taken to task by an eminent musical critic in England for not always singing high-class music. The reply of the singer was, "If I sing nothing but high-class music I must starve, for unless I sing some of the popular stuff I get no applause, if I get no applause I get no engagements, and if I get no engagements I must starve; I have therefore determined to sing one high-class number for my reputation's sake, and then any 'tommy rot' the public requires." Now, ladies and gentlemen, the environment supplied by this Society will never induce the performer to produce 'tommy rot' —to use that inelegant expression.

I venture to state that no musician not affected by the blindness of conceit or overweening egotism could attend the Society's meetings regularly without deriving very great advantage, that is, if he has got the breadth of mind and the capacity to receive it. An instrumentalist must profit by hearing a good vocalist, and vice versa; the opportunity of hearing new works before we purchase and study them ourselves is a great advantage, and a large acquaintance with the classics, whether for voice, violin, piano, or any combination of instruments, must widen conception and re-act favourably on artistic effort.

It has been contended that members should be paid for their services at our meetings, but as members of this Society are all professionals, it would be scarcely gracious to ask our professional brother to pay us for an occasional performance. Moreover, directly you introduce the practice of paying the members for their services at the monthly meetings you create a spirit that will eventually lead to competition for fees, and so probably destroy the fine spirit which should and does now permeate the whole Society. Surely, ladies and gentlemen, the twentieth century members of the Society are not going to be less unselfish, less interested in musical talent and the musical art of their country than were the nineteenth century members? You may be quite sure that Sir Edward Elgar and Sir A. C. Mackenzie,

=== Page 9 ===
whose names are famous throughout the musical world, are not launching the Musical League for any selfish motives, and if we allow the fair name of the M.S.V. to become tarnished by the breath of selfishness, we shall deserve and probably receive the condemnation of the whole profession. To me it is most inspiring and most gratifying to remember that in every effort in connection with this Society's meetings the members are working unselfishly and in the spirit of true brotherhood for each other and the community.

Ruskin has said, "You may be, after you have made your money, misers and usurers; you may be, after you have got your fame, jealous, proud, wretched and base; but yet, as long as you won't spoil your work, you are artists." Without disputing the dictum of Ruskin, I may be permitted to express the opinion that an artist who allows himself to be jealous, proud, wretched, base, or miserly has spoilt his character as a man, is devoid of gratitude to his Maker, and has lost all nobility, sympathy, dignity and true spirit of manhood, and, believe me, his work will lack sincerity whenever he seeks to portray those qualities.

All honour be given to those members, past and present, who most generously have acknowledged the gratitude they owe to their country and fellow citizens, and who, when opportunity offered, were ever willing, without fee or hope of reward, to do what they could for the uplift and advance of musical art or the welfare of the whole musical profession. Let us remember that all that we are and all that we have gained is due to the gift with which we are endowed, to the environment, and to the great musicians whom we shall never be able to repay. As far as fame, position and wealth are concerned, do we not owe it all, or nearly all, to our country or countrymen? By becoming members of this Society we can stimulate each other to greater exertion in the cause of art, can keep the community in some degree in touch with the most advanced musical thought, protect the musical profession from legislative injustice, and the selfish designs of grasping and greedy tyranny, and in this manner pay back some of the debt we owe to our country, and show our sincere gratitude for those heavenly compositions which the great masters have left us to use and enjoy. Unless a member is touched by some such influence, his membership is not likely to do himself or the Society much good, and unless some of the white heat of enthusiasm is to be found in our midst the Society must become decadent and moribund.

In conclusion, I trust that all members will live up to the dignity of their membership, and in the spirit of true brotherhood, not alone for what they can get out of the Society, but also for what they can do for each other, and for the art whereby they live and have their being.
