MELBOURNE LIEDERTAFEL.

THE FOLLOWING IS

The Scheme.

A fanfare of trumpets will announce the entrance of His Excellency the Governor, and subsequently the National Anthem will be sung by the LIEDERTAFEL.

PART I.

CHORAL BALLAD - Landerkennung - Edvard Grieg.
SOLO BARITONE—HERR LOUIS FLEISSNER.

THE LIEDERTAFEL.

AND it was Olav Trygvason,
Sailing on the north sea wide,
Bearing the hope to found a kingdom,
Far on the other side
Yonder the cliffs appearing,
Like a battlement loom, their dark crest rearing,
And it was Olav Trygvason,
Found he ne'er a spot to land,
E'en like the waves the royal desire,
Shattered upon the strand.
"See!" cried the bard unleeping,
"Yonder snow covered-peaks o'er cloud bands peeping."

And it was Olav Trygvason,
Suddenly he seemed to sight
Towering temples, domes and spires,
Glist'ning in virgin white.
Then vowed the king undaunted,
With his followers to tread that land enchanted,

=== Page 8 ===
Onward he went; the rushing streams
Heralded the coming spring;
Swayed in the stormy wind the forest strangely was murmuring,
Sounds as of church bells chiming,
And then spake the king,
Spake as tho' dreaming:
"Here the spot to found our kingdom!
Hell! these temple walls defy thee!
Hearts are swelling, souls are yearning,
God, to thee alone be glory.

"Be my faith as yonder mountains,
Root as deeply, shine as purely;
And as these my faith strive upwards,
On to him, the All Creator."
Olav's prayer be ours to utter,
As we near the throne of mercy.
Hearts are swelling, souls are yearning,
God to thee, alone be glory
    On to God! On to God!

Song - - Unspoken Words - - Gastaldon.
Miss Kitty Grindley.

Part Songs (without an Accompaniment)—

(a) Last Night - - Kjenulf.
Soloist—Mr. H. J. Robinson.

(b) 'Neath the Ship's gay 'broidered awning
(From The Pearl of Brazil) F. David.

THE LIEDERTAFEL.

(a) Last night the nightingale 'woke me,
    Last night when all was still,
It sang in the golden moonlight
From out the woodland hill.

=== Page 9 ===
I open'd the window so gently,
I look'd on the dreaming dew,
And oh! the bird, my darling, was singing,
    Singing of you, of you,
    Yes, singing of you.

I think of you in the day time,
I dream of you by night,
I wake and would you were here, love,
And tears are blinding my sight.
I hear a low breath in the lime tree,
The wind is floating through,
And oh! the night, my darling,
Is sighing, sighing of you, of you,
    Is sighing of you.

Oh think not I can forget you;
I could not if I would,
I see you in all around me—
The stream, the night, the wood,
The flowers that slumber so gently,
The stars above the blue,
Oh! Heaven itself my darling,
Is praying, praying for you, for you,
    Is praying for you.

(b) 'NEATH the ships' gay 'broidered awning,
    Brightly coloured by the setting sun,
Let us drink, friends, 'till day's dawning,
Wine that makes the blood more swiftly run;
And let each repeat again, again,
The hardy sailors' say refrain.
'Neath the ships' gay 'broidered awning,
Comrades drink here 'till day's dawning;
To the Admiral give all homage,
Laud his glory, and his courage;
Let the crew with one accord,
To-day some pledge of love afford;
Of courage, courage, let us sing,
To work this same true spirit bring.
Ah! yes, through wine's aid all danger scorning,
Comrades, comrades, drink here till day's dawning.

SCENA—Ed stria E strano è strano
(La Traviata) Verdi.

MADAME SCHREIBER.

MILA

By ROBERT SCHWALM,
Op. 38.

(Translated from the German by SIEGFRIED PINCUS, Fitzroy.)

This work is the gift of the Conductor.

ROBERT SCHWALM'S Mila is essentially a product of the modern German school of thought. The original text has been done into English for the Liedertafel by Mr. Siegfried Pincus, one of the Performing Members. Mila will be presented for the first time in Australia this evening.

The fragrance of the rose, breathing sweetness and laden with love and longing, fades and dies—if its dainty petals be wantonly culled and scattered even so, this superb tone-picture tells its own wondrous love-tale, an ideal romance of a fierce conflict betwixt human passion and superhuman power—it is a canvas wedded to colours of such a dazzling brilliancy and exquisite beauty as to defy description and scorn dissection.

Associated Ladies' Choir and Liedertafel.

OLAF TRYGVASON, a descendant of Harold Haarfager (the first king of Norway), was brought up at the court of Gardarike (Russia), and was baptized in England while on a Viking raid. When he learned that Norway was dissatisfied with her king (a scion of an older branch of the same race), he resolved upon conquering the kingdom and converting the people to Christianity. Richly endowed by nature, the superior of all his contemporaries in bodily and spiritual gifts, and so comely to behold that none had seen his like; he was revered in the middle ages as the noblest example of the Norman race; he conquered more by his personality than by his sword. He commenced his struggle in the Drontheim district, and the beginning of this conflict is depicted in the scenes of the drama here set to music.

The action takes place in an ancient Norman temple in the Drontheim district, at the time of Olav Trygvason's appearance. Period—End of the tenth century.
