Into the silence of the monotonous Steppes of Middle Asia come the first echoes of a peaceful Russian song. In the distance sound the trampling of horses and camels and the strange cries of the Orient. Under the protection of the Russian flag it moves safely on its weary journey through the immeasurable waste. It recedes further and further. The songs of Russia and the wild notes of the East merge into the solemn harmony of nature, slowly sinking into the echoes of the eternal Steppes.

"FRITHJOF."

The story of Frithjof the Bold and Ingeborg the Fair is told in "Frithjof's Saga," a poem in twenty-four cantos, by Esaias Tegner, published in 1820.

The hero, Frithjof, is son to Thorsten Vikingsson, who, though King Bele's best warrior and chosen friend, is of lowly origin. Frithjof the Strong and Bold dares to love Fair Ingeborg, daughter of his king and descendant of great Odin. Bele looks kindly on young Frithjof's love, but death takes from the youth at once a monarch and a friend. When Frithjof subsequently demands the hand of Ingeborg from Helge, son and successor of Bele, he is rejected with scorn. Later, old King Ring, a mighty monarch of the North, would make fair Ingeborg his queen, but Helge, influenced by soothsayers, sends a refusal, accompanied by a taunting message from brother Halfdan, to "Old Grey-beard." King Ring incensed, declares war against the brothers, and Ingeborg is sent for safety to the vale of Baldur, the god of innocence and virtue. While in the sacred grove, the maiden receives a visit from her lover, the joy of which is followed by the fear that she has angered the gods by keeping a love-tryst in that holy place. At a council of war assembled at Bele's cairn, Frithjof again demands from Helge the hand of Ingeborg, in return for which he offers his best aid in the impending battle. Helge again repels him, and forces him to make the fatal admission that he has visited Ingeborg in the sacred grove. He thereupon denounces him as a profaner of temples, and orders him to do penance for his sacrilege by sailing to the Orkneys to wring from fierce Earl Agantyr long-withheld tribute. Frithjof tries to persuade Ingeborg to fly with him, but in vain. Giving her his arm-ring, he departs, promising to return and make her his bride.

Borne through his perilous voyage by his charmed ship "Ellida," Frithjof returns at length to his native land to find his estates laid waste with fire and sword, and the fair Ingeborg given in marriage to victorious King Ring. He learns that at the marriage ceremony the cowardly Helge tore from his sister's arm her lover's ring, and hung it upon the arm of Baldur's image. Frithjof, furious, seeks out the King whilst he is in the act of offering sacrifice at Baldur's pyre. He flings Agantyr's tribute full in Helge's face, and, hastily snatching at the arm ring, he overturns the huge idol upon the sacred fire. The flames lap it about, and, leaping upward with angry roar, spread to the temple and the sacred grove, which are soon reduced to ashes.

Frithjof is now shunned by all as a perpetrator of sacrilege; he is banished from his country, and, sailing away in the "Ellida," he devotes himself to a life of wild adventure. The hero's troubled life is ultimately crowned with happiness. After many years he returns to his native land, and succeeds in gaining the goodwill of King Ring, who at his death bequeaths him his kingdom and his queen.

Bruch, Max, musical composer, born at Cologne 6th January, 1838, received his first musical instruction from his mother (nee Almenrider), who was a highly-esteemed teacher of music, and who often in her young days sang at the Rhenish musical festivals. At the age of eleven Bruch, then a pupil of Karl Breidenstein, attempted compositions on a large scale, and at the age of fourteen he had already brought out a Symphony at Cologne. From 1853 to 1857 he held the Mozart Scholarship at Cologne, and in that capacity he was a special pupil of Ferdinand Hiller in the theory of music and composition; and of Karl Reinecke (till 1854), and of Ferdinand Breunung in playing the piano. After a short stay in Leipzig, he resided from 1858 to 1861 as musical teacher at Cologne, and was very assiduous in composing. On the death of his father, in 1861, he set out on an extensive tour of study, which, after brief stays at Berlin, Leipzig, Vienna, Dresden, and Munich, ended at Mannheim, where his opera "Lorelei" (after the text written by Geibel for Mendelssohn) was produced in 1863. At Mannheim also, between 1862 and 1864, he wrote the chorus-works, "Frithjof," "Römischer Triumphgesang," "Gesang der heiligen drei Könige," and "Flucht der heiligen Familie." In 1864-65 he was again on his travels, visiting Hamburg, Hanover, Dresden, Breslau, Munich, Brussels, and Paris. Then he brought out his "Frithjof" with extraordinary success at Aix-la-Chapelle, Leipzig, and Vienna. From 1865 to 1867 he was musical director at Coblenz, and from 1867 to 1870 director of the court choir at Sonderhausen. At Coblenz he wrote, among other things, his well-known first concerto for the violin, and at Sonderhausen two symphonies and portions of a Mass. The opera "Hermione," which was produced in 1872 at Berlin, where Bruch resided from 1871 to 1873, had only a succès d'estime. The chorus "Odysseus" likewise belongs to the period of the composer's residence at Berlin. After he had been five years (1873-78) at Bonn, devoting his time exclusively to composing "Arminius," "The Song of the Bell," and his second concerto for the violin, and after he had paid two visits to England for the purpose of producing some of his works, he became in 1878, on the resignation of Hockhausen, Director of Stern's Singing Academy at Berlin; and in 1880 he was nominated to succeed Sir Julius Benedict as Director of the Philharmonic Society at Liverpool.
